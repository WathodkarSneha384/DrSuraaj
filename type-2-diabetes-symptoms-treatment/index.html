Type 2 Diabetes: Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment & Long-Term Management

Type 2 diabetes is common and manageable. Here's how it develops, the signs to watch for, how it's diagnosed and the treatments, diet and habits that keep it under control.

Introduction: What is Type 2 Diabetes?

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic metabolic disorder in which the body cannot regulate blood glucose (blood sugar) effectively. It develops when the body's cells become less responsive to insulin, a hormone produced by the pancreas, and over time the pancreas may also become unable to produce enough insulin to meet the body's requirements.

Insulin plays an essential role in maintaining normal blood glucose levels. It helps glucose move from the bloodstream into muscle, liver and fat cells, where it can be used or stored for energy.

When insulin does not work effectively, glucose accumulates in the bloodstream, resulting in high blood sugar levels.

Type 2 diabetes is one of the most common metabolic disorders worldwide. It can affect adults as well as younger individuals, and its incidence is strongly associated with obesity, physical inactivity, genetic susceptibility and metabolic dysfunction.

The important thing to understand is that type 2 diabetes is manageable. Early diagnosis, appropriate medical treatment, nutrition, physical activity and regular monitoring can significantly reduce the risk of complications and improve quality of life.

What happens inside the body? Understanding insulin resistance

To understand type 2 diabetes, it is important to understand insulin resistance.

Insulin is produced by beta cells in the pancreas. After eating, carbohydrates are digested into glucose, which enters the bloodstream. Rising blood glucose stimulates insulin secretion.

Insulin then signals cells to take up glucose and helps the liver regulate glucose production.

In insulin resistance, the body's cells respond less effectively to insulin.

As a result:

  • Muscle cells take up less glucose
  • The liver continues releasing glucose even when it is not required
  • The pancreas produces additional insulin to compensate
  • Over time, pancreatic beta cells may lose their ability to maintain adequate insulin production
  • Blood glucose levels gradually increase

This process can begin years before diabetes is formally diagnosed.

Clinical significance: Insulin resistance may coexist with abdominal obesity, high triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, hypertension and fatty liver disease. These conditions often form part of a broader metabolic dysfunction.

Causes and risk factors of type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes usually develops due to a combination of genetic, metabolic, environmental and lifestyle factors.

Not everyone who develops diabetes is overweight, and not everyone with obesity develops diabetes. Individual susceptibility plays an important role.

Major risk factors

1Abdominal obesity

Excess fat around the abdomen, particularly visceral fat surrounding internal organs, is associated with insulin resistance.

Visceral adipose tissue is metabolically active and releases inflammatory mediators and fatty acids that can interfere with insulin signalling.

2Family history and genetics

People with a parent or sibling who has type 2 diabetes have a higher risk of developing the condition.

Genetic factors influence pancreatic beta-cell function, insulin secretion and insulin sensitivity.

3Physical inactivity

Lack of exercise reduces glucose utilisation by skeletal muscles and contributes to insulin resistance.

Regular physical activity improves insulin sensitivity, even without significant weight loss.

4Unhealthy dietary habits

Frequent consumption of:

  • Sugar-sweetened beverages
  • Refined carbohydrates
  • Highly processed foods
  • Excess calories
  • Foods rich in saturated and trans fats

may contribute to weight gain and metabolic dysfunction.

5Fatty liver disease

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously called non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), frequently coexists with insulin resistance.

Excess fat accumulation in the liver can contribute to increased hepatic glucose production and metabolic disturbances.

6Increasing age

The risk generally increases with age, although type 2 diabetes is increasingly diagnosed in younger adults and adolescents.

7Other associated conditions

  • Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
  • Hypertension
  • High triglycerides
  • Low HDL cholesterol
  • Previous gestational diabetes
  • Sleep disturbances and obstructive sleep apnoea

Early symptoms of type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes may develop gradually over several years. Many individuals have no obvious symptoms during the early stages.

Some people discover they have diabetes during routine health examinations.

Common symptoms

1Increased thirst (polydipsia)

High blood glucose increases the amount of glucose filtered by the kidneys. When glucose exceeds the renal threshold, it may spill into urine and increase water loss.

This leads to dehydration and increased thirst.

2Frequent urination (polyuria)

Excess glucose in the urine pulls water along with it through osmotic diuresis, causing frequent urination.

3Increased hunger (polyphagia)

Despite elevated blood glucose, cells may not utilise glucose efficiently. Some individuals experience increased hunger.

4Unexplained weight loss

When glucose cannot be utilised effectively, the body may break down fat and muscle for energy, resulting in weight loss.

5Fatigue and weakness

Poor glucose utilisation, dehydration and associated metabolic disturbances can contribute to persistent tiredness.

6Blurred vision

Changes in blood glucose can alter fluid balance in the eye lens, causing temporary changes in vision.

7Recurrent infections

Some individuals experience recurrent skin infections, urinary tract infections or genital fungal infections.

8Slow wound healing

Persistent hyperglycaemia can impair immune function and circulation, contributing to delayed wound healing.

9Darkening of skin around the neck

A condition called acanthosis nigricans causes thickened, dark, velvety skin, particularly around the neck, armpits and groin.

It is frequently associated with insulin resistance.

Important: Absence of symptoms does not rule out diabetes. Screening is particularly important in people with risk factors.

How is type 2 diabetes diagnosed?

Diabetes is diagnosed through laboratory blood glucose testing.

The three commonly used diagnostic tests are:

  • HbA1c
  • Fasting plasma glucose (FPG)
  • Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

Diagnostic criteria

TestPrediabetesDiabetes
HbA1c 5.7–6.4% ≥6.5%
Fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL ≥126 mg/dL
2-hour 75 g OGTT 140–199 mg/dL ≥200 mg/dL

These are ADA diagnostic thresholds for non-pregnant individuals. Local clinical guidelines and individual circumstances should also be considered.

HbA1c test

HbA1c measures the percentage of haemoglobin molecules that have glucose attached to them. It reflects average blood glucose exposure over approximately the previous three months.

It is useful for both diagnosing diabetes and monitoring long-term glucose control.

However, HbA1c may be misleading in certain situations, including some types of anaemia, altered red blood cell turnover, haemoglobin variants and pregnancy. In these cases, plasma glucose-based testing may be more appropriate.

When should diabetes diagnosis be confirmed?

In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycaemia, two abnormal test results are generally required to confirm the diagnosis.

For example, a patient with HbA1c of 7.2% but no symptoms may require repeat testing or another abnormal diagnostic test.

Important investigations after diagnosis

Diagnosing diabetes is only the first step. A comprehensive medical assessment helps identify associated metabolic abnormalities and existing complications.

A physician may recommend:

InvestigationClinical purpose
HbA1c Long-term glucose control
Fasting and post-meal glucose Glucose assessment
Lipid profile Cardiovascular risk
Kidney function (creatinine, eGFR) Renal health
Urine albumin-creatinine ratio Early diabetic kidney disease
Liver function tests Associated liver abnormalities
Blood pressure Cardiovascular risk
Dilated retinal examination Diabetic eye disease
Foot examination Neuropathy and vascular problems
Vitamin B12, when indicated Particularly with long-term metformin use or neuropathy

The frequency of investigations depends on the patient's age, diabetes duration, treatment, clinical findings and associated risk factors.

Treatment of type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes management is individualised.

There is no single medicine or treatment plan that is appropriate for every patient.

The treatment strategy depends on:

  • HbA1c level
  • Age and duration of diabetes
  • Body weight
  • Kidney and liver function
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Heart failure
  • Associated metabolic conditions
  • Risk of hypoglycaemia
  • Patient preferences and affordability

A. Lifestyle modification

Lifestyle modification is the foundation of diabetes management.

It should continue even when medications are prescribed.

B. Oral antidiabetic medications

1Metformin

Metformin is a commonly prescribed first-line glucose-lowering medication.

Its primary actions include:

  • Reducing hepatic glucose production
  • Improving insulin sensitivity
  • Reducing fasting blood glucose

It generally has a low risk of hypoglycaemia when used alone and is usually weight neutral or associated with modest weight reduction.

Kidney function must be assessed before prescribing, and gastrointestinal side effects can occur.

2SGLT2 inhibitors

Examples include empagliflozin and dapagliflozin.

These medications reduce glucose reabsorption in the kidneys, increasing glucose excretion through urine.

Their important clinical benefits include:

  • Glucose reduction
  • Modest weight reduction
  • Blood pressure reduction
  • Heart failure risk reduction in appropriate patients
  • Kidney protection in selected patients

They are particularly important in patients with type 2 diabetes and relevant cardiovascular or kidney disease.

Potential adverse effects include genital fungal infections, volume depletion and rare but serious euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis.

3DPP-4 inhibitors

Examples include sitagliptin, linagliptin and vildagliptin.

They increase the activity of incretin hormones, helping regulate insulin secretion and glucagon release in a glucose-dependent manner.

They generally have:

  • Low hypoglycaemia risk when used alone
  • Weight-neutral effects
  • Modest glucose-lowering efficacy

4Sulfonylureas

Examples include glimepiride and gliclazide.

These medications stimulate insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells.

They can effectively lower blood glucose but may cause hypoglycaemia and weight gain.

5Thiazolidinediones

Pioglitazone improves insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues.

It may be useful in selected patients but requires caution because of fluid retention, weight gain, fracture risk and contraindications such as symptomatic heart failure.

C. Injectable medications

GLP-1 receptor agonists

These medicines mimic the action of the naturally occurring GLP-1 hormone.

Examples include semaglutide and liraglutide.

Their actions include:

  • Increasing glucose-dependent insulin secretion
  • Suppressing inappropriate glucagon secretion
  • Slowing gastric emptying to varying degrees
  • Increasing satiety and reducing appetite

Some agents produce substantial weight loss and have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits in appropriate populations.

Common adverse effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation.

Insulin therapy

Insulin may be required when:

  • Blood glucose is significantly elevated
  • HbA1c is very high
  • There are symptoms of marked hyperglycaemia
  • There is unexplained catabolic weight loss
  • Other medications are insufficient
  • There is acute illness or another clinical indication
Good to know: Insulin is not a treatment failure. It is an important and sometimes essential treatment for diabetes.

The role of nutrition in diabetes management

Nutrition is one of the most important components of diabetes care.

A diabetes-friendly diet does not mean completely avoiding carbohydrates or eating only boiled food. The objective is to improve glucose control, maintain a healthy body weight, preserve muscle mass and reduce cardiovascular risk.

A. Choose the right carbohydrates

Carbohydrates have the greatest direct effect on post-meal blood glucose.

Prefer:

  • Whole wheat roti
  • Oats
  • Millets such as jowar and bajra
  • Brown rice or appropriately portioned regular rice
  • Pulses and legumes
  • Vegetables
  • Whole fruits rather than fruit juices

Reduce frequent consumption of:

  • Sugar-sweetened beverages
  • Sweetened tea and coffee
  • Bakery products
  • Refined flour products
  • Sweets and desserts
  • Large portions of refined carbohydrates

The total carbohydrate quantity and its distribution throughout the day matter, not just the type of food.

B. Importance of protein

Protein supports muscle maintenance, tissue repair and metabolic health.

Suitable sources include:

  • Eggs
  • Fish
  • Chicken
  • Milk and curd
  • Paneer
  • Dal and legumes
  • Soy products

Protein requirements should be individualised, particularly in people with chronic kidney disease.

C. Healthy fats

Include appropriate quantities of:

  • Nuts and seeds
  • Almonds and walnuts
  • Flaxseeds and chia seeds
  • Fish containing omega-3 fatty acids
  • Unsaturated vegetable oils

Limit trans fats and excessive saturated fats.

D. Dietary fibre and gut health

Dietary fibre helps improve satiety, bowel function and post-meal glucose responses.

Good sources include:

  • Green leafy vegetables
  • Beans and pulses
  • Whole grains
  • Fruits with edible skin
  • Seeds

E. Practical Indian diabetes plate

A simple meal-planning approach:

Plate portionFood
½ plate Non-starchy vegetables and salad
¼ plate Protein: dal, paneer, egg, fish or chicken
¼ plate Roti, rice or other whole grains

This is a practical starting point, not a rigid prescription. Individual requirements vary according to nutritional status, activity, medication and kidney function.

Exercise and physical activity

Regular physical activity improves insulin sensitivity and helps skeletal muscles use glucose more effectively.

Exercise also supports weight management, cardiovascular health and mental well-being.

Recommended physical activities

1Walking

Start with 20–30 minutes of brisk walking, gradually increasing according to fitness and medical condition.

Walking after meals may help reduce post-meal glucose excursions.

2Resistance training

Strength training helps maintain and build skeletal muscle, which is an important tissue for glucose utilisation.

Examples

  • Squats
  • Resistance band exercises
  • Weight training
  • Wall push-ups
  • Chair exercises

3Aerobic exercise

Cycling, swimming, brisk walking and other aerobic activities improve cardiovascular fitness.

4Reduce sedentary time

Avoid sitting continuously for several hours. Take short movement breaks during prolonged sitting.

A commonly recommended target for adults is at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, along with resistance training on 2–3 days when appropriate.

Patients using insulin or sulfonylureas should receive guidance on exercise-related hypoglycaemia prevention.

Can type 2 diabetes be reversed?

This is one of the most frequently asked questions by patients.

The medical term commonly used is type 2 diabetes remission, rather than permanent cure.

Some individuals with type 2 diabetes can achieve remission, particularly when diagnosed relatively early and when significant weight loss is achieved.

Remission is generally defined as HbA1c below 6.5% for at least three months without glucose-lowering medication.

Factors that may support remission

  • Significant and sustained weight reduction
  • Reduction in visceral and liver fat
  • Structured nutritional intervention
  • Regular physical activity
  • Improvement in insulin sensitivity
  • Appropriate medical supervision

Some individuals achieve remission through intensive lifestyle interventions, while others may require metabolic surgery depending on their clinical circumstances.

However, remission is not guaranteed. Blood glucose can rise again, and regular monitoring remains essential.

Please note: Patients should never discontinue prescribed diabetes medication without medical supervision.

Long-term complications of uncontrolled diabetes

Persistently elevated blood glucose can damage blood vessels, nerves and several organs.

Complications are broadly divided into microvascular and macrovascular complications.

A. Microvascular complications

1Diabetic retinopathy

Damage to small blood vessels in the retina can cause visual impairment and blindness if not detected and managed.

Regular retinal examinations are important, even when vision appears normal.

2Diabetic nephropathy

Diabetes can damage the filtering units of the kidneys.

Early kidney disease may have no symptoms. Urine albumin-creatinine ratio and estimated glomerular filtration rate help detect kidney involvement.

3Diabetic neuropathy

Damage to peripheral nerves may cause:

  • Burning sensations in the feet
  • Tingling
  • Numbness
  • Reduced sensation
  • Pain
  • Balance difficulties

B. Macrovascular complications

1Coronary artery disease

Diabetes increases the risk of atherosclerosis, heart attacks and other cardiovascular events.

2Stroke

Damage to blood vessels increases the risk of cerebrovascular disease.

3Peripheral arterial disease

Reduced blood circulation in the legs can contribute to foot ulcers, infections and poor wound healing.

Prevention of complications

The most effective approach includes:

  • Individualised blood glucose control
  • Blood pressure management
  • Cholesterol management
  • Smoking cessation
  • Regular eye examinations
  • Kidney function assessment
  • Foot care
  • Appropriate cardiovascular risk reduction

How often should a person with diabetes be monitored?

Diabetes is a long-term condition requiring regular follow-up.

ParameterGeneral monitoring frequency
HbA1c Every 3 months if treatment changes or goals are not achieved; at least twice yearly if stable
Blood pressure At routine clinical visits
Lipid profile At baseline and periodically according to risk and treatment
Kidney function At least annually, more frequently when indicated
Urine albumin-creatinine ratio At least annually in type 2 diabetes
Eye examination At diagnosis and periodically thereafter
Foot examination At least annually; more often according to risk
Weight and waist circumference During regular follow-up

The monitoring schedule should be adjusted according to disease duration, complications, treatment and individual risk.

When should you consult a physician?

Consult a physician if you experience:

  • Increased thirst and frequent urination
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Persistent fatigue
  • Recurrent infections
  • Blurred vision
  • Darkened skin around the neck
  • A family history of diabetes with additional metabolic risk factors
  • Repeatedly elevated blood glucose readings
🚨 Seek urgent medical attention Severe dehydration, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep or rapid breathing, confusion, altered consciousness or severe weakness can indicate a serious metabolic emergency. Do not delay emergency assessment.

Frequently asked questions about type 2 diabetes

Can type 2 diabetes be cured permanently?

Type 2 diabetes can sometimes enter remission, particularly following substantial and sustained weight loss. However, remission is not equivalent to a guaranteed permanent cure. Continued monitoring is necessary.

Can a person with diabetes eat rice?

Yes. Rice does not need to be completely eliminated. Portion control, food combinations, total carbohydrate intake and individual glucose responses are important.

Is fruit harmful for diabetic patients?

No. Whole fruits can be part of a balanced diabetes diet. Portion size matters. Whole fruit is generally preferable to fruit juice.

Does walking reduce blood sugar?

Yes. Physical activity increases muscle glucose utilisation and improves insulin sensitivity. Walking after meals may help control post-meal glucose levels.

Is insulin required for every diabetic patient?

No. Many patients can initially manage their condition using non-insulin medications and lifestyle interventions. Insulin becomes necessary in certain clinical situations.

Can a thin person develop type 2 diabetes?

Yes. Genetic susceptibility, visceral fat, ectopic fat accumulation and impaired insulin secretion can contribute to diabetes even in people who do not appear overweight.

What is the ideal HbA1c for a diabetic patient?

An HbA1c below 7% is a commonly used target for many non-pregnant adults. However, targets should be individualised based on age, comorbidities, hypoglycaemia risk and life expectancy.

Does diabetes affect sexual health?

Yes. Long-standing diabetes can affect blood vessels, nerves and hormonal health, contributing to erectile dysfunction and sexual health problems. Good metabolic control and appropriate medical evaluation can help.

Can diabetes cause fatty liver?

Yes. Type 2 diabetes and MASLD frequently coexist because of shared mechanisms involving insulin resistance, visceral fat and abnormal lipid metabolism.

How can I prevent diabetes if I have prediabetes?

Weight management, regular physical activity, appropriate nutrition, adequate sleep and regular glucose monitoring can reduce the risk of progression to type 2 diabetes.
